In the monopoly model examined so far in this chapter, the price set by the monopolist is <i>linear</i>: each unit of the good is sold at the same price, regardless of how many units are purchased or what type of buyer is involved. In this section, we extend the analysis by assuming that the monopolist can charge different prices for the same good, depending on the quantity purchased or the characteristics of the consumer. As we will see, this practice — known as <b>price discrimination</b> — allows the monopolist to capture a larger share of the surplus generated by the exchange.

There are several forms of discrimination, and here we will examine two:

<ul> <li> <p> <span class="marginnote"> We will soon explore which pricing strategies the monopolist can adopt to implement this type of discrimination. </span> <b>Perfect price discrimination</b>, which occurs when the monopolist is able to charge a different price for each unit sold — a price equal to the maximum willingness to pay among consumers for that unit — thereby capturing the <i>entire</i> surplus; </p> </li> <li> <p> <span class="marginnote"> Classic examples include discounted fares for young people and students (e.g. FrecciaYoung), or for software and digital services (e.g. Spotify Premium or Amazon Prime). The price is differentiated based on an observable characteristic, such as age or student status. </span> <b>Discrimination based on observable characteristics</b>, which consists of applying different prices to distinct groups of consumers, identifiable based on observable features (such as age or place of residence), but treated uniformly within each group. </p> </li> </ul> <br> <h2 id="subsec_discrimination-perfect">Perfect Price Discrimination</h2>
When the monopolist applies linear pricing, all units are sold at the same price, and a consumer buys an additional unit only if their willingness to pay for that unit is at least equal to that price. The monopolist could increase the quantity sold, but to do so would have to lower the price — and this would reduce the revenue on units already sold. This is the <i>price effect</i> discussed <a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/it/II/5/1#mon-price-effect">earlier</a>. To avoid this, the monopolist prefers to keep a higher price, foregoing the sale of additional units that consumers would have purchased at a lower price still above marginal cost.

With perfect price discrimination, the monopolist overcomes this constraint: they sell each unit at a price equal to the highest willingness to pay for that unit — the highest price some consumer is willing to pay to obtain it. The monopolist does not need to lower the price of previous units and can thus sell a greater quantity
<span class="marginnote">
Mathematically, while under linear pricing marginal revenue is given by $MR = P \times (1 + 1/E^D)$, under perfect discrimination it simply becomes $MR = P$.
</span>
without experiencing the price effect. As a result, <i>the price effect disappears — in other words, the marginal revenue function coincides with the demand curve</i>. This implies that the firm captures the entire surplus and thus has an incentive to maximize it: the traded quantity is the competitive one, and deadweight loss is zero.

<h2 id="subsec_discrimination-twopart">Two-Part Tariffs and Bundling</h2>
Perfect price discrimination may seem, at first glance, difficult to achieve in practice. Are we really suggesting that the monopolist is able to charge a different price depending on how many units the consumer has <i>already</i> purchased? While this may seem surprising, there are several ways in which the monopolist can obtain exactly the same result, without explicitly applying a different price for each unit. We now examine two pricing strategies, both quite common in reality, that can achieve the same effect: the two-part tariff and bundling.

A <b>two-part tariff</b> consists of two components:

<ul> <li>a <i>fixed access fee</i>, paid <i>once</i> by the consumer to be able to access the good or service;</li> <li>a <i>unit price</i>, paid for each unit purchased.</li> </ul> The two-part tariff that maximizes the monopolist’s profit is based on a very simple idea: set the unit price so that the consumer is induced to buy all the units that generate surplus, and set the fixed fee so as to capture all that surplus. To do this, the monopolist sets the unit price equal to marginal cost, so that the consumer is encouraged to purchase every unit for which their willingness to pay exceeds the cost. At that point, the monopolist sets a fixed fee equal to the net benefit the consumer would receive if paying only the unit price. The consumer is willing to pay this fixed fee, because they receive the quantity they want at a total price that is still convenient; but in the end, their surplus is zero, and all the value generated by the exchange goes to the firm.

Let’s return to the example in Figure 5.6, where each unit represents a subway ticket. A consumer is willing to pay 7 euros for the first ticket, 6 for the second, and so on, down to 1 euro for the seventh. If the unit price is set equal to marginal cost ($1.50$ euros), the consumer purchases 6 tickets. The surplus they would receive by paying only the unit price is:

<p><span style="color: Black;">\(\begin{gathered} (7 - 1.5) + (6 - 1.5) + \dots + (2 - 1.5) = 27 - 9 = 18 \end{gathered}\)</span></p>
The monopolist can therefore charge a fixed access fee of 18 euros, in addition to 1.50 euros per ticket. The total profit will be 18 euros, just as under perfect discrimination.

In the example from Figure 5.7, where the good consists of mobile data (measured in gigabytes) sold by a mobile operator, the individual demand is $P = 5 - Q$ and marginal cost is $MC = 1$. If the unit price is set at 1 euro, the consumer will purchase 4 GB. The surplus obtained is 8. Once

One possibility is to <i>segment the market into groups identifiable on the basis of observable characteristics such as age or geographical location</i>, and apply a different linear price to each group. All consumers within the same group pay the same price, but prices may vary across groups.

In many contexts, this form of discrimination combines with the strategies discussed earlier. For instance, two-part tariffs can be offered separately for young people and adults, or differentiated bundles for private customers and businesses. In these cases, segmentation by observable groups integrates with nonlinear pricing, bringing the outcome even closer to that of perfect discrimination.
